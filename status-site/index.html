Suggested demo route

The descent is one continuous take; there is no chapter-select. A rehearsal run that shows everything without stalling looks like this:

  1. 0 → 17 % — Lab and neural structure. Establishes the fixed camera and the powers-of-ten cadence.
  2. 24 → 50 % — Cell. The dissolve work of the last week is most visible here.
  3. 48 → 60 % — NirA protein and MET169. The CH4 redox cycle runs on its own 30-second clock; let it play at least one full loop.
  4. 75 → 87 % — the long dark approach. Deliberately near-empty.
  5. 90 → 95 % — nucleons resolve out of a single glowing point. This is the slowest passage in the show, by design.
  6. 95 → 100 % — quarks.

A full descent at full trigger is roughly 33 seconds of pure travel time. In practice the operator holds on beats, so plan for several minutes.

3 — Technical foundation

Engine and cluster

  • Unreal Engine 5.7, custom plugin QZoom (13,276 lines of C++; the show pawn alone is 5,529).
  • nDisplay, two cluster nodes: Node_Wall and Node_Floor, one viewport each, mesh projection onto Display_Wall / Display_Floor (1500 × 845 each). Wall pitch 0°, floor pitch −90° — the L-shaped Deep Space geometry.
  • Render sync over ethernet; input replicated from the primary node. Show state (zoom depth, orbit, menu selections, reaction clock) is broadcast as cluster events so both nodes scrub the same frame.
  • Local rehearsal setup: both nodes run as separate processes on one workstation (RTX 4090).

The central idea: a fixed camera and a world that scales

The camera never moves. Each station is scaled around its own pivot by exp((depth − centre) × K), so a constant trigger rate produces a constant number of orders of magnitude per second — the natural Powers-of-Ten cadence. Two consequences worth knowing in the room:

  • Whatever sits exactly on a station's pivot is what you fly into. Anything offset gets multiplied outward by the scale and leaves the frame.
  • Station positions are not authored by hand — they are log-spaced from a table of real physical sizes.

The MET169 chapter and the CH4 redox cycle

The scientific core of the piece. Methionine-169 in the NirA protein carries a sulfur atom that acts as a redox switch. Two enzyme partners visit it on a 30-second loop:

TimeEvent
0 – 2 scarrier (FmoB) fades in
10.8 sdocks — delivers
12 – 14 scollector (MsrA) fades in
21 – 23 scarrier has arrived at its destination and fades out
25.8 srelease — collects
28 – 30 scollector fades out, finishing exactly at the loop end

The motion comes from a Sequencer track that is scrubbed by the reaction clock rather than played — so it freezes when the station is off screen and inherits the descent's own pacing. The presence windows are aligned to the animation's key times, not to guessed phases: the carrier's fade-out begins the moment it stops moving.

All NirA geometry was exported MET169-centred — the residue sits on the coordinate origin by construction, and the protein body is positioned around it. Any re-alignment therefore moves the protein, never the methionine.

Brightness deserves a note for Monday: it is driven from a Material Parameter Collection, which means Markos can keyframe the glow of the methionine, the carrier and the collector independently on an existing Sequencer track — met_bright, car_bright, col_bright — without touching code.

The probability-volume shader

The MET169 hull carries a purpose-built master with five registers, switchable live and blendable between neighbours: Rim (Fresnel), Cloud (drifting 3D noise, density rather than membrane), Heatmap (viewing thickness through a spectral ramp), Iso-bands (contour lines of density — the scientific register), and Point cloud (stipple density as probability of presence). Rim is the current default. This is the visual language question worth putting to Karl and Alejandro on Monday.

What was actually costing frames — and what it turned out to be

Two findings from the last two weeks are worth repeating, because both were counter-intuitive and both cost real time to locate.

The “grainy dissolve” was never the dissolve

For weeks the fade in and out of every station showed a fine, sandpaper-like grain. Frequency tuning, detail-noise amplitude, LOD dithering, filler particles, z-fighting and the drill cone were all investigated and all ruled out. The actual cause was structural: a masked material with noise added to its opacity mask and a clip value is a dither by construction. The fade was the source. The fix was to let temporal anti-aliasing resolve it (DitherTemporalAA), rolled out across the cell and structure masters — and, for the cell, an alternative genuinely translucent material with no threshold at all.

GPU particle systems need authored bounds

The orbital filler particles were intermittently visible on the wall or the floor but never both, and later disappeared entirely. The cause was not culling settings, not materials, not the cluster configuration and not multi-GPU. GPU-simulated Niagara emitters cannot compute their own bounds; they fall back to a fixed box whose default is ±100 local units. At the fillers' component scale that is ±1 and ±0.1 world units — boxes that fail almost every per-view frustum test, the smaller one first. The pawn now sets real bounds at runtime, because the editor-side setting is not serialised.

6 — Tooling and workflow

The project is driven far more by scripted editor operations than by hand-editing. 521 Python scripts sit in dev/, each one a single, documented operation executed against the running editor over a remote bridge — material construction, asset import, actor placement, tag management, measurement probes, and C++ source patches.

Two working rules have emerged from expensive mistakes and are now standard:

  • Measure, then act. Probe scripts print the state before any change and verify it afterwards. Several long detours in this project came from acting on a plausible theory instead of a measurement.
  • Version, never delete. Deleting a referenced material instance once silently reset fifteen mesh slots to the default grid material. New versions get new names.

Source control is Git with LFS for binary assets; 131 commits, with the Quantum Zoom work beginning in late May 2026.

Karl Jansen has already asked once for “quantum computer” to be replaced by “quantum system” in public-facing text. Worth carrying that care into the rehearsal narration: the piece visualises a quantum system — a molecule and its nucleus — and the computational work behind it is a means, not the subject.
